A good chair

Nearly every great architect has tried to design one, and most of us spend more of the day in one than anywhere else. It looks simple. It's the hardest small thing there is.

“A chair is a very difficult object. A skyscraper is almost easier.”

Attributed to Ludwig Mies van der Rohe

It has to hold a body that keeps moving, survive being tipped back on two legs, look right from every side, and cost what people will pay. Here's a century of people trying.

A century of sitting

1859

No. 14

Michael Thonet · steam-bent beech

Six pieces of wood, ten screws, two nuts. Shipped flat, thirty-six to a one-metre crate, and assembled on arrival. Some fifty million were made by 1930. You have sat in a café chair descended from it.

1918

Red and Blue

Gerrit Rietveld · painted wood

Two planes floating on a frame of sticks, none of them quite touching. Less a chair than an argument about what a chair could be. Rietveld painted it in primary colours a few years later.

1925

Wassily

Marcel Breuer · bent steel tube and canvas

Breuer was twenty-three, and the story goes that he was looking at his bicycle's handlebars. Steel tube that bicycle makers knew how to bend made furniture light, strong and suddenly industrial.

1929

Barcelona

Mies van der Rohe and Lilly Reich · steel and leather

Made, the story goes, for the King and Queen of Spain to sit in, once, at the German pavilion of a world's fair. Two crossed curves of steel that have been in office lobbies ever since.

1932

Paimio

Alvar Aalto · bent birch plywood

Designed for a tuberculosis sanatorium in Finland. The seat is a single sheet of plywood curled at both ends, set at an angle Aalto meant to open a patient's chest and make breathing easier.

1946

LCW

Charles and Ray Eames · moulded plywood

During the war, the Eameses moulded plywood leg splints for the US Navy. Afterwards they used the same trick on furniture: a low, friendly chair in five bent pieces, joined by rubber that lets the back give a little.

1967

Panton

Verner Panton · moulded plastic

One continuous S of plastic, with no legs at all, after years of trying to find a material that could do it. The first chair moulded in a single piece. It looks like 1967 because it more or less invented how 1967 looked.

1970s

Monobloc

Nobody in particular · injection-moulded plastic

Plastic pellets in, a finished chair out in about a minute, for the price of a sandwich. It stacks, it survives the rain, and it is very probably the most common chair on Earth. Designers love to hate it.

1994

Aeron

Bill Stumpf and Don Chadwick · mesh and aluminium

No foam at all: a stretched mesh that lets air through and spreads your weight. Sold in three sizes, like shoes. It became the uniform of the dot-com office, and then of every office.

18591994

Your back has opinions

In the 1960s and 70s, Alf Nachemson and Gunnar Andersson put pressure sensors into volunteers' spinal discs and measured them sitting, standing and slouching. Sitting bolt upright turned out to load the lower back more than standing. Leaning back eased it, and a little support in the small of the back eased it more.

So: feet flat, thighs about level, a backrest that meets the curve of your lower spine, and room to recline. Drag the backrest and see.

What I look for

A good chair disappears. An hour in, you've stopped thinking about your back, and that's the whole trick. The best posture, the physiotherapists say, is your next one. So I want a chair that lets me move, and I still try to get up from it every so often.
