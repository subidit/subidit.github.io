Fountain pens

A pen that carries its own ink and lets it down by gravity and capillary action. You don't press. You let it write.

Dip, write, dip

For about five thousand years, writing meant dipping. Egyptian scribes cut reeds; medieval monks cut goose feathers and re-cut them every few pages as the tips wore soft. In the nineteenth century, the factories of Birmingham began stamping out steel nibs by the million, and writing got cheap. But every few words, you still had to stop and dip.

Putting the ink inside the pen was the obvious answer and a stubborn problem. Early reservoir pens either starved or flooded, and usually leaked on the way. The trouble wasn't the ink. It was the air.

The pen breathes

As ink leaves a sealed reservoir, it leaves a vacuum behind it, and the vacuum holds the rest of the ink back. A pen that works has to let air in at the same rate ink goes out.

That's the feed: the ridged black piece under the nib. A narrow channel carries ink down by capillary action, and a wider one lets bubbles of air climb up to take its place. The fins along its sides catch any surplus when your hand warms the pen and the air inside expands.

Lewis Waterman patented his three-channel feed in 1884. The story goes that he was an insurance salesman who lost a big contract when a leaky pen blotted it. It's a lovely story, and historians doubt it.

Anatomy of a nib

Write something

An italic nib is a chisel of metal held at an angle. Pull it sideways and the line is thin; pull it down and it's broad. Every letter's thicks and thins come from the direction of the hand, nothing else.

Ink that darkens

For more than a thousand years, serious writing was done in iron gall ink: oak galls crushed and soaked, mixed with iron sulfate and a little gum. It goes on a pale blue-grey and turns black over the next hours as the iron oxidises in the paper. Leonardo filled his notebooks with it. Bach wrote his scores in it, and some of them are now being slowly eaten by it, because the same acid that bites into the page to make the ink permanent keeps biting.

Modern fountain pen inks are mostly dyes in water: gentle on pens, and available in more colours than anyone needs. Good ones shade, pooling darker where the nib slows down, so the writing keeps a record of the hand. Try the iron gall above and watch it darken.

Why bother

The ballpoint won, of course. László Bíró patented his in 1938, and by the 1950s a ballpoint cost next to nothing. It's a fine tool for signing a delivery.

A fountain pen asks for a little care: refill it, keep it capped, use decent paper. In return it glides with no pressure at all, and it makes your handwriting look like you meant it. I find it hard to write a careless sentence with one.
