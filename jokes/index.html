A joke that lands

Made with care counts here too. A joke has fewer parts than a watch, and every one of them has to be in exactly the right place.

Four thousand years of the same jokes

  1. Sumer, about 1900 BC

    Something which has never occurred since time immemorial: a young woman did not fart in her husband's lap.

    The oldest joke anyone has found. Nobody is quite sure why it was funny, but it is, unmistakably, a fart joke.

  2. Greece, about the 4th century AD

    A talkative barber asked a man, “How shall I cut your hair?” “In silence.”

    From the Philogelos, the “laughter-lover”, the oldest joke book that survives: some 265 jokes, many about a hapless egghead who could be the hero of a sitcom today.

  3. The internet, 2002

    Two hunters are out in the woods when one of them collapses. His friend calls the emergency services: “My friend is dead! What can I do?” The operator says, “Calm down. First, let's make sure he's dead.” There's a silence, then a gunshot. “OK, now what?”

    The winner of LaughLab, a year-long experiment in which people around the world submitted and rated tens of thousands of jokes to find the funniest.

Taking one apart

A joke builds a picture in your head, then shows you that the same words made a different picture all along. Tap the last word.

I told my wife she was drawing her eyebrows too high. She looked .

First you hear how she felt about it. Hurt, maybe. A little startled.

Then you see her face. Eyebrows drawn that high make anyone look permanently astonished. The punchline was a description all along.

  • Setup paints the picture and plants the word that matters.
  • The pause lets the first picture settle.
  • Punchline as short as possible, with the switch at the very end.

Why we laugh, according to people who thought hard about it

Superiority
Thomas Hobbes, 1650s. We laugh at someone else's misfortune or stupidity, because for a moment we feel above it. Explains the egghead jokes, and a lot of playgrounds.
Relief
Herbert Spencer, then Sigmund Freud. A laugh lets off nervous energy that built up around something we're not supposed to say. Explains why the oldest joke is about a fart.
Incongruity
Kant, then Schopenhauer. We expect one thing, get another, and for a split second the mismatch makes sense. The theory most comedians would recognise.
Benign violation
Peter McGraw and Caleb Warren, 2010. Something seems wrong, threatening or rude, and at the same time it's fine. Too safe and it's dull; too wrong and it's just upsetting.

Your timing

Here's a room. You deliver the punchline. The only thing that's yours to judge is the pause.

What I've learned

Economy: a setup that doesn't announce itself, a turn you didn't see coming, and not one word more than it needs. Timing is a mechanism, like a watch's escapement, and the pause does most of the work. And it's the cheapest well-made thing there is. You can give it away without losing it.
