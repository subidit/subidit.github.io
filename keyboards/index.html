Mechanical keyboards

The thing I touch more than anything else I own. Go on, type something. Or tap the keys.

Switch

Three ways to press a key

Under each keycap is a switch: a plastic stem riding on a steel spring, with two metal contacts that touch partway down. What you feel depends on what the stem meets on the way.

Linear
Nothing. A smooth, rising push all the way down. Fast, quiet, favoured by gamers.
Tactile
A small bump just before the key registers, so your finger knows it's done and can let go early.
Clicky
The bump, plus a crisp click. Delightful to you, and a reliable way to annoy everyone nearby.

Five parts

  1. Keycap Sculpted so each row tilts a little differently, and your fingers meet every key flat. The best have their letters moulded right through, in a second colour of plastic, so they can never wear off.
  2. Stem The moving part. Its shape decides the feel: smooth sides for linear, a bump for tactile, a separate jacket that snaps for clicky.
  3. Top housing Keeps the stem straight. Any wobble here is the cheap rattle you hear in a bad keyboard.
  4. Spring Sets how hard you press. A typical switch registers at around 45 grams, two millimetres down, out of four.
  5. Bottom housing Holds the metal contacts, and pins that solder to the circuit board. Or, these days, push into sockets so you can swap switches without a soldering iron.

A short history of pressing things

  1. 1868

    Christopher Latham Sholes patents a typewriter. Its keys end up in the QWERTY order, which has outlived the typewriter by most of a century.

  2. 1983

    A German company called Cherry introduces its MX switch. Its cross-shaped stem becomes the standard that nearly every keycap still fits.

  3. 1985

    IBM's Model M arrives, with a buckling spring under every key that snaps sideways with a loud, unmistakable clack. Many are still in daily use.

  4. 1990s

    Rubber domes take over. Cheaper, quieter, mushier. For most people, a keyboard becomes something that comes free with the computer.

  5. 2010s

    Hobbyists bring the switch back: custom boards, hand-tuned springs, and keycap sets made in small batches that people wait a year for.

Why it matters

I spend hours a day pressing keys. A good keyboard reports every press clearly, in the finger and in the ear, and it makes the most repetitive thing I do feel a little bit like playing an instrument.
