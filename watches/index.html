Watches

A small machine that does one job, slowly, for years. It has no battery and no signal, only a spring, a few wheels and a heartbeat, and it is right now keeping time.

Lost at sea

For most of history, a ship's navigator could find latitude from the height of the sun, but not longitude. That needed a clock. The Earth turns fifteen degrees an hour, so if you know the time at home and the time where you are, the difference tells you how far east or west you've sailed. A clock four seconds wrong puts you a nautical mile off.

Pendulum clocks were useless on a rolling deck. In 1714 Parliament offered £20,000 for a practical answer, and a self-taught carpenter named John Harrison spent most of his life on it. His fourth timekeeper, H4, was barely bigger than a pocket watch. On its trial voyage to Jamaica in 1761 it lost about five seconds in eighty-one days.

How it keeps time

A spring, wound tight

Everything starts with a ribbon of steel coiled inside a drum called the barrel. Winding the crown, or the swing of your arm turning a weighted rotor, tightens it. Fully wound, a typical mainspring holds enough energy for about forty hours.

A train of wheels

Left alone, the spring would unwind in a second. A chain of gears, each driving a smaller pinion, slows it and divides its turning into hours, minutes and seconds. The fourth wheel turns exactly once a minute; put a hand on its arbor and you have a seconds hand.

The escapement

This is the clever part. A pallet fork locks the escape wheel, then lets it slip forward half a tooth at a time, the tick and the tock. Each time, the tooth gives the fork a tiny push, which it passes on to keep the balance swinging. The lever escapement was invented by Thomas Mudge around 1755, and nearly every mechanical watch still uses it.

The balance, the heartbeat

A weighted wheel on a hair-thin spiral spring swings back and forth at a steady rate, whatever the spring's tension. Most modern watches beat 28,800 times an hour: four full swings, eight ticks, every second. Get this wrong by one part in ten thousand and the watch drifts nine seconds a day.

A quartz crystal vibrates 32,768 times a second. That's two to the fifteenth power, so fifteen halvings in a row give exactly one pulse a second, and the motor steps the hand once.

Seventeen rubies

The “jewels” on a movement aren't decoration. They're bearings: tiny drilled rubies that the steel pivots turn in, because ruby is hard, smooth and barely wears. Since Auguste Verneuil worked out how to grow them in a flame in 1902, they've cost almost nothing. A typical hand-wound movement uses seventeen, one for each place that friction matters.

Christmas Day, 1969

Seiko put the Astron on sale in Tokyo, the first quartz wristwatch. It cost as much as a small car and kept time to within five seconds a month, far better than anything mechanical. Within fifteen years quartz was cheap, and the Swiss industry, which had owned the craft for two centuries, nearly died.

It came back as something different. A mechanical watch is now a thing you choose rather than need, made for people who like knowing there's a small, patient machine on their wrist.

Accuracy is overrated

An officially certified chronometer may gain six seconds a day or lose four. Your phone is far better. A good mechanical watch drifts a few seconds a day, and checking how many is half the fun.
